## What AUM means

**AUM — assets under management** — is the total market value of everything a
mutual fund scheme holds, less its liabilities: all the shares, bonds and cash
in the portfolio, marked to today's prices. It is the size of the pot.

The [first mutual fund post]({% post_url 2026-09-25-what-a-mutual-fund-is %})
defined NAV as that pot divided by the number of units. So:

```
AUM  =  NAV per unit × Units outstanding
```

which means AUM changes for two entirely different reasons: units change
(investors buy or redeem) or NAV changes (the market moves). Fund marketing
tends to present AUM growth as evidence of investor confidence. Often it's
just the index going up.

headline like "AUM fell 4%" or "AUM grew 6%" is uninformative until you know

**net inflow** — AMFI publishes it monthly, by category — not AUM.

## Where size genuinely matters

**Fees.** SEBI caps what an open-ended scheme can charge, in slabs that fall as
AUM rises. The logic is that running a ₹20,000 crore fund doesn't cost forty times
what running a ₹500 crore fund does. Under the [SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/regulations/apr-2026/securities-and-exchange-board-of-india-mutual-funds-regulations-2026_100744.html),
in force from 1 April 2026, the cap is on the **base expense ratio** (BER), and for open-ended
equity-oriented schemes other than index funds, [ETFs]({{ '/2026/10/13/index-funds-vs-etfs/' | relative_url }}) (exchange-traded funds) and [funds of funds]({{ '/2026/10/20/international-funds/' | relative_url }})
it is, per the daily net assets in each slab ({{ au.ber_regulation }}):

| {{ s.aum_slab }} | {% if s.max_ber_note %}{{ s.max_ber_note }}{% else %}{% assign tp = s.max_ber_pct | append: '' | split: '.' %}{{ tp[0] }}.{{ tp[1] | append: '00' | slice: 0, 2 }}%{% endif %} |{% endfor %}

Index funds and ETFs are capped at {{ au.ber_cap_index_etf_pct | append: '0' }}% of daily net assets regardless of size.
The base expense ratio leaves out statutory levies and transaction costs, and a scheme may charge brokerage
on top of it, up to {% assign x = au.ber_brokerage_cash_pct %}{% include inr.html n=x %}% of trade value in the cash market and
{% assign x = au.ber_brokerage_derivatives_pct %}{% include inr.html n=x %}% in derivatives. So the total expense ratio an investor
sees can sit above the base figure. Before 1 April 2026 the 2018/19 framework capped the *total*
expense ratio, starting at {% assign x = au.ter_slabs_equity[0].max_ter_pct %}{% include inr.html n=x %}% on the first ₹500 crore, so the two sets of numbers
are not directly comparable. The shape is the same: the cap falls as the fund grows. The practical
consequence is that as a fund grows, its maximum permitted fee *falls*. Whether the fund actually charges less is a
different question — the cap is a ceiling, not a price — and the
[expense ratio post]({% post_url 2026-09-28-expense-ratios-direct-vs-regular %})
is where to check what you're really paying.

**Liquidity.** A large fund can't buy meaningful positions in small companies
without moving their prices. A ₹50,000 crore small-cap fund putting 2% into a
company with a ₹1,000 crore [market cap]({% post_url 2026-09-27-market-cap %}) would own the whole company. This is why very large
small- and mid-cap funds sometimes stop accepting lump sums, and why "biggest
fund in the category" is not a compliment in every category.

**Nothing else.** AUM says nothing about returns, risk or quality. The
[rolling returns]({% post_url 2026-09-27-rolling-returns %}) and
[drawdown]({% post_url 2026-09-29-drawdown %}) posts don't mention size once,
and that's not an oversight.

AUM is what everything in a shop's storeroom is worth.

That number goes up when new stock arrives (inflows). It also goes up when

the shop says "our storeroom is worth 6% more!", ask: new stuff, or just
higher prices?

## Common mistakes

- **Reading AUM growth as inflows.** Separate the two. In a year the index
  rose 6%, an unchanged fund's AUM rose 6%.
- **Treating big as safe.** Size buys lower fee caps and nothing else. A large
  fund fell exactly as far in 2008 as a small one holding the same stocks.
- **Ignoring size where it bites.** In small- and mid-cap categories, AUM
  relative to the size of the companies held is a real constraint.
- **Comparing AUM across categories.** A ₹5,000 crore liquid fund is small;
  a ₹5,000 crore small-cap fund is large. Same number, opposite meaning.

**Takeaway:** AUM is NAV times units — the market value of a fund's pot — and
it moves with the market whether or not anyone invests a rupee. It sets the
regulator's fee ceiling and constrains what a small-cap fund can buy; it says
nothing about whether the fund is any good.
