## From price to momentum

Everything so far has looked at price directly — where it went, where it
stalled, what its [average]({% post_url 2026-10-12-moving-averages %}) is doing, and how
much [volume]({% post_url 2026-10-13-volume %}) came with it. **Oscillators** ask a different question:
not *where* is the price, but how *fast* has it been moving, and is that pace
sustainable?

The **RSI — Relative Strength Index**, devised by J. Welles Wilder in 1978 —
is the most widely used of them. It compresses recent price action into a
single number between 0 and 100, measuring how much of the recent movement
has been upward versus downward.

Note the name is misleading: RSI has nothing to do with strength *relative to
another stock* or to an index. It's entirely self-contained — a stock
compared only to its own recent behaviour.

## The formula

```
             100
RSI = 100 − ───────           RS = Average Gain / Average Loss
            1 + RS                 over N periods (N = 14 by default)
```

The averaging is Wilder's smoothing — a running average that weights recent
periods more heavily, equivalent to an exponential average with
α = 1/N.

Work through what the formula does:

- If every one of the last 14 days rose, average loss is zero, RS is infinite,
  and RSI is 100.
- If every day fell, average gain is zero, RS is zero, and RSI is 0.
- If gains and losses have been equal on average, RS is 1 and RSI is 50.

So RSI is a bounded measure of how one-sided recent movement has been. That's
all it is. Everything else is interpretation laid on top.

Imagine you're sprinting. Someone measures how hard you've been running over

A score of 90 means you've been sprinting flat out almost the whole time. And
the sensible thought is: *you probably can't keep this up.*

But "probably can't keep it up" is not the same as "is about to stop." Some
runners keep sprinting for a very long time. If you bet on every tired-looking
runner stopping immediately, you'd lose a lot of bets.

## The conventional interpretation

| RSI | Conventional label |
|---|---|
| Above 70 | "Overbought" |
| Below 30 | "Oversold" |
| Around 50 | Neutral |

The words *overbought* and *oversold* do a lot of damage, because they sound
like statements about value. They aren't. "Overbought" does not mean too
expensive — it means the price has risen persistently for a couple of weeks.
A great business in a strong uptrend will be "overbought" for months at a
stretch, and it isn't wrong; it's just rising.

Read those three rows carefully, because they are the honest summary of what
this indicator is.

In July 2024 RSI crossed 70 and the stock climbed another 12% over the next
three months. Anyone acting on "overbought" then would have exited near the
start of the best stretch in the dataset.

In September 2024 RSI crossed 70 again — at a nearly identical reading — and
this time the stock fell 26% over the following five months. Same signal,
opposite outcome.

And in November 2024 RSI dropped to {{ r.oversold_but_kept_falling.rsi }}, deeply "oversold," and the price
fell another 9% before it finally turned.

There is no way to tell these apart *at the time*. That isn't a failure of
this particular dataset — it's the well-known central weakness of oscillators:
in a strong trend, they stay pinned at an extreme, and the extreme reading is
a description of the trend rather than a warning about it.

## Divergence

The more interesting use of RSI, and the one experienced practitioners
usually prefer, is **divergence** — when price and RSI disagree.

- **Bearish divergence**: price makes a higher high, RSI makes a lower high.
  The new peak was reached with less momentum than the last one.
- **Bullish divergence**: price makes a lower low, RSI makes a higher low.
  The new trough came with less downward force.

The logic is more defensible than the overbought/oversold reading, because
it's a comparison between two moments rather than a fixed threshold applied
blindly. It is also considerably harder to use: divergences can persist for
a long time before anything happens, and they're much easier to identify
after the fact than during.

## Doing it in Python

Getting the smoothing right matters — a plain rolling mean is *not* Wilder's
method and will give you different numbers:

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("britannia-ohlcv-2024-04-to-2026-03.csv",
                 parse_dates=["date"]).set_index("date")

delta = df.close.diff()
gain = delta.clip(lower=0)
loss = -delta.clip(upper=0)

n = 14
avg_gain = gain.ewm(alpha=1/n, adjust=False).mean()   # Wilder's smoothing
avg_loss = loss.ewm(alpha=1/n, adjust=False).mean()

df["rsi"] = 100 - 100 / (1 + avg_gain / avg_loss)
print(df.rsi.iloc[14:].describe().round(1))     # note: skip the warmup
```

That `.iloc[14:]` is not cosmetic. The very first RSI value in this dataset
computes as exactly **100.0**, purely because no down-day had occurred yet in
the window. It's an artefact of having no history, not a reading. Every
indicator has a warmup period and quoting values from inside it is a real
mistake, not a technicality.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading "overbought" as "expensive."** It means the price has risen
  steadily for a fortnight. Nothing more.
- **Acting on threshold crossings in a strong trend.** This is the failure
  mode the table above documents three times over. In trends, oscillators
  pin.
- **Using values from the warmup window.** The first RSI reading here is
  100.0 and means nothing at all.
- **Assuming a rolling mean is Wilder's smoothing.** They give different
  numbers, and much published RSI code gets this wrong.
- **Tuning the period until the signals look good.** RSI(14) is Wilder's
  convention. RSI(9) or RSI(21) will look better on data you've already seen,
  which is exactly the problem.
- **Treating divergence as a timing tool.** Divergences can run for months.
  They describe a loss of momentum, not an appointment.

**Takeaway:** RSI measures how one-sided the last fourteen sessions have
been, on a 0–100 scale, and that's genuinely useful information about pace.
But "overbought" is a statement about speed, not value — and in this dataset
the same reading above 70 preceded a further 12% rise once and a 26% fall
another time, which is roughly all you need to know about using the threshold
on its own.
