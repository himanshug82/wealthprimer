## What an NFO is

An **NFO — new fund offer** — is the first time a mutual fund scheme sells
units. For a short window the fund collects money at a fixed price per unit,
usually ₹10 ([the investor page]({{ nf.sebi_investor_url }}) of SEBI, the
Securities and Exchange Board of India, says units
are sold "at a fixed price, usually ₹10 per unit during the NFO period").
After the window closes, units are allotted, the fund invests the money, and
from then on it's an ordinary fund with a daily
[NAV]({% post_url 2026-09-25-what-a-mutual-fund-is %}) (net asset value, the
per-unit value of the fund).

The rules, from SEBI's [Master Circular for Mutual Funds]({{ r.mc26.url }})
({{ r.mc26.date | date: "%-d %B %Y" }}) and the
[{{ r.regs26.name }}]({{ r.regs26.url }}), in force from
{{ r.regs26.in_force | date: "%-d %B %Y" }}:

| Rule | What it says | Where |
|---|---|---|
| How long an NFO stays open | At least {{ nf.min_working_days }} working days, at most {{ nf.max_days }} calendar days (ELSS — equity-linked savings schemes — follow government guidelines) | Master Circular, paragraph {{ nf.mc_para }} |
| Allotment | Units allotted, or money refunded, within {{ nf.allot_working_days }} working days of the NFO closing | Master Circular, paragraph 1.7.4 |
| Putting the money to work | Within {{ nf.deploy_business_days }} business days of allotment, extendable by another {{ nf.deploy_extension_days }}; after that, investors must be told they can exit without an exit load | Master Circular, paragraph {{ nf.deploy_para }} (from circular {{ nf.deploy_circular }}) |
| Who pays for the launch | "All the expenditures pertaining to launch of new fund offer till the date of allotment" are borne by the AMC (asset management company), trustees or sponsor | {{ nf.expenses_reg }} of the 2026 Regulations |

That last row matters: launch costs — advertising, distribution, the offer
documents — aren't charged to your units. An NFO isn't *more expensive* to buy
into than an existing fund. The problem with the ₹10 isn't cost. It's the
story told about it.

## The formula

The whole question comes down to the arithmetic from the
[what a mutual fund is post]({% post_url 2026-09-25-what-a-mutual-fund-is %}):

```
Units you get       =  Amount invested ÷ NAV at purchase
Value later         =  Units × NAV later

So:  Value later    =  Amount invested × (NAV later ÷ NAV at purchase)
```

The NAV *level* cancels out. Only the percentage change in NAV matters, and
that depends on what the portfolio does — not on whether the NAV started at
₹10 or ₹1,000.

## So what *is* different about a new fund?

Not the price. These things:

- **No track record of its own.** A new index fund at least has the index's
  history. A new *active* fund has none — only the manager's record on other
  funds, which is a different portfolio. Everything the
  [rolling returns]({% post_url 2026-09-27-rolling-returns %}) and
  [drawdown]({% post_url 2026-09-29-drawdown %}) posts asked you to check
  simply doesn't exist yet.
- **Why it's being launched.** SEBI allows only one scheme per category per
  fund house, except index funds and ETFs (exchange-traded funds) on different indices, funds of funds
  with different underlying funds, and sectoral or thematic funds on different
  themes (Master Circular, paragraph 3.10.1). So a new active NFO is usually
  one of those exceptions — often a theme — or a fund house filling a
  category it didn't have. In our view it's worth asking why that theme is
  being offered *now*.
- **A small fund at first.** Expense-ratio caps fall as a scheme grows. Under
  {{ nf.ber_reg }} of the 2026 Regulations, an actively managed open-ended
  equity-oriented scheme may charge a base expense ratio (before statutory
  levies) of up to {{ nf.ber_first_pct }}% on its first ₹500 crore, stepping
  down to {{ nf.ber_last_pct }}% on assets above ₹50,000 crore. A new fund
  starts in the top slab. What it actually charges is in the factsheet —
  check it after launch, not just the NFO document.
- **Money waiting to be invested.** For up to {{ nf.deploy_business_days }} business days after
  allotment (longer with the extension) part of the money may not yet be in
  the portfolio the fund was sold on.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking ₹10 is a "ground-floor price".** It's a starting unit size, not
  a valuation. The table above is two funds that ended within a fraction of a
  percent of each other despite a {{ n.nav_ratio }}× difference in NAV.
- **Buying an NFO because an existing fund "has already gone up".** The older
  fund's past rise is already in its NAV; it's neither a head start nor a
  handicap for anyone buying today. The new fund starts from the *same
  market*.
- **Treating the launch campaign as information.** A heavily advertised NFO
  tells you a fund house wants to raise money for that idea now. It says
  nothing about how the idea will do.
- **Skipping the checks because there's nothing to check.** No track record
  is a reason for *more* caution about an active fund, not less. For a new
  index fund, look at the index's history and the fund house's tracking
  record on its other index funds.

**Takeaway:** A new fund's ₹10 NAV is a unit size, not a price tag — the NAV
level cancels out of every return you'll ever earn. A ₹10 Nifty 50 fund
launched in {{ n.first_nav_date | date: "%Y" }} and a ₹{{ ur.nav_start | round }} one tracking the same index ended within a few
hundred rupees of each other on ₹1 lakh; what a new fund really lacks is a
track record.
