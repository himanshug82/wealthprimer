## The second dimension

Every chart so far in this series has been about price. But each bar carries
a second number that most beginners scroll past: **volume**, the number of
shares that changed hands.

Price tells you where buyers and sellers agreed. Volume tells you how many
of them were involved in the agreement. A 3% rise on a quiet Tuesday and a 3%
rise on the heaviest trading day of the year are the same move on a line
chart and very different events.

The standard framing is that volume measures *conviction*. That's a decent
intuition, but it needs one correction immediately, because it's the thing
almost everyone gets wrong.

## Every trade has two sides

You will constantly read that "buying volume" pushed a stock up, or that
"selling volume" drove it down. Taken literally this is nonsense: every share
bought was sold by someone. Volume is not a measure of buyers exceeding
sellers, because buyers can never exceed sellers. They match exactly, always.

What volume actually measures is **participation** — how much the asset
changed hands. High volume means many people revised their view enough to act
on it. It says nothing, by itself, about which direction they were leaning.

Hold onto that, because the chart below makes the point better than any
argument could.

Imagine a school playground where kids swap trading cards.

The **price** is how many sweets one card is worth today. The **volume** is
how many cards actually got swapped.

If one card gets swapped and the price jumps, maybe two kids were being
silly. If four hundred cards get swapped and the price jumps, something real
happened — a rumour that the card is being discontinued, maybe.

But here's the catch: for every kid who *got* a card, another kid *gave one
away*. So a busy day doesn't mean everyone wanted to buy. It means everyone
wanted to *trade*. Those are different things.

</details>

## The conventional readings

The usual claims about volume, stated as most sources state them:

| Pattern | Conventional reading |
|---|---|
| Price rises on rising volume | Move has participation behind it |
| Price rises on falling volume | Fewer participants; move may be running out |
| Price falls on rising volume | Selling has conviction behind it |
| Volume spike at an extreme | Possible exhaustion — the last of the crowd acting |
| Breakout on heavy volume | More persuasive than a breakout on light volume |

That last row is the one with the most practical use. A price pushing through
a [resistance zone]({% post_url 2026-11-12-support-and-resistance %}) on triple the usual
volume means a lot of people participated in that decision. The same
breakout on thin volume could be a handful of orders in a quiet session.

Now here is where the conventional readings meet reality.

Look at **27 March 2025**. It is by a wide margin the largest volume day in
the dataset — {{ ta.volume_spikes[2].x_avg }} times the average — over 67 lakh shares against a
median day of about 2,96,000. If volume signalled direction, something

The close moved **−0.2%**.

Enormous participation, no price change. Something moved a very large number
of shares between holders that day — an index rebalancing, a block deal, a
large fund repositioning — and the price barely noticed, because the buying
and selling were matched by design.

Contrast it with **12 November 2024**: 6.7 times average volume and a −7.5%
day. Same category of signal on a volume screen; completely different event.

Volume tells you *how many*. It does not tell you *which way*. You need the
price to tell you that, and you need the news to tell you why.

## Volume in Indian markets: two practical cautions

**Delivery volume matters.** Indian exchanges publish delivery percentage —
the share of traded volume that resulted in actual delivery rather than
being squared off intraday. A day with huge volume but 20% delivery is mostly
day traders passing shares around; 70% delivery means people took real
positions. NSE and BSE both publish this, and it's more informative than raw
volume.

**Volume is exchange-specific.** A stock listed on both NSE and BSE has
separate volume on each. Charts usually show one. It's rarely a problem for
large caps, where NSE dominates, but it's worth knowing what your chart is
counting.

## Doing it in Python

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("britannia-ohlcv-2024-04-to-2026-03.csv",
                 parse_dates=["date"]).set_index("date")

df["vol_avg50"] = df.volume.rolling(50).mean()
df["vol_ratio"] = df.volume / df.vol_avg50
df["day_move"]  = df.close.pct_change() * 100

spikes = df[df.vol_ratio > 3]
print(spikes[["volume", "vol_ratio", "close", "day_move"]].round(2))
```

The always-useful comparison is volume *relative to its own recent average*,
never volume in absolute terms. Half a million shares is enormous for one
stock and a quiet morning for another.

## Common mistakes

- **Talking about "buying volume" and "selling volume."** Every trade has a
  buyer and a seller. Volume measures participation, not the balance of
  intent.
- **Reading a volume spike as directional.** As 27 March 2025 shows plainly,
  the largest volume day in two years moved the close by −0.2%.
- **Comparing raw volume across stocks.** Only the ratio to that stock's own
  average means anything.
- **Ignoring index rebalancing, block deals, and expiry days.** These
  generate enormous volume for structural reasons that have nothing to do
  with anyone's view of the company.
- **Forgetting the delivery percentage.** High volume with low delivery is
  churn, not accumulation.
- **Expecting volume to confirm on demand.** Plenty of genuine, durable moves
  happen on unremarkable volume. Absence of a volume spike is not evidence
  against a move.

**Takeaway:** Volume measures how many shares changed hands, which is
participation — not the balance of buyers over sellers, because that balance
is always exactly zero. Compare it to the stock's own recent average, use it
to judge how many people were involved in a move, and remember the largest
volume day in this entire dataset moved the price by two-tenths of a percent.
