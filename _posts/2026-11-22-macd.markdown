## Turning two averages into one line

The [moving averages post]({% post_url 2026-11-16-moving-averages %}) ended on a
trade-off: a fast average reacts quickly and gives false alarms, a slow one
is reliable and late. The **MACD — Moving Average Convergence Divergence**,
built by Gerald Appel in the late 1970s — takes that tension and makes it the
measurement itself.

Instead of watching two averages and waiting for them to cross, MACD plots
the *gap between them* as a single line. When the fast average is far above
the slow one, the gap is large and positive — the recent trend is
accelerating away from the longer-term one. When they converge, the gap
shrinks toward zero. When the fast falls below the slow, it goes negative.

So MACD is a momentum measure derived entirely from moving averages. That
makes it a lagging indicator built out of lagging indicators, which is worth
holding in mind throughout.

## The formula

```
MACD line   = EMA(12) − EMA(26)              the gap between fast and slow
Signal line = EMA(9) of the MACD line        a smoothed version of that gap
Histogram   = MACD line − Signal line        the gap between those two
```

Three components, each one derived from the one before it:

| Component | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **MACD line** | How far the 12-day average has pulled away from the 26-day |
| **Signal line** | The MACD's own recent average — slower, smoother |
| **Histogram** | Whether the MACD is pulling away from, or falling back toward, its own average |

The 12, 26 and 9 are Appel's original settings and have no special
significance beyond convention — they roughly correspond to two weeks, a
month, and a week and a half of trading days back when markets traded six
days a week.

<summary>🧒 Explain it like I'm 10 <em>(optional — skip if this is already clear)</em></summary>

Two people are walking the same route: a fast walker and a slow one.

The **MACD line** is simply how far ahead the fast walker is. When they're
racing off, the gap grows. When they get tired, the gap shrinks. If they fall
behind the slow walker, the gap goes negative.

The **signal line** is the average gap over the last little while. And the
histogram is whether today's gap is bigger or smaller than that recent
average — in other words, whether the fast walker is pulling away or fading.

None of this tells you where the road goes. It only tells you about the
walkers.

</details>

## The three readings

Practitioners look at MACD three ways:

1. **Signal-line crossovers.** MACD crossing above its signal line is read as
   bullish; below, bearish. This is the most commonly used and, as we're
   about to see, the noisiest.
2. **Zero-line crossings.** MACD crossing above zero means the 12-day average
   has crossed above the 26-day — a slower, more meaningful shift.
3. **Divergence.** Price makes a new high, MACD doesn't. Same logic as
   [RSI divergence]({% post_url 2026-11-20-rsi %}).

Thirty-one signals in under two years — roughly one every three weeks. And
they cluster: look at the dense groups in mid-2025 and again around the turn
of 2026, where crossovers arrive within days of each other in alternating
directions.

That clustering is **whipsaw**, and it's the defining problem with
crossover-based indicators. When price moves sideways, the fast and slow
averages sit almost on top of each other, and tiny wiggles flip the gap back
and forth. Every flip is technically a signal. Most of them are noise about
noise.

Notice also *when* the whipsaws happen: precisely during the ranging periods
when there's no trend to catch. The indicator is quietest and most useful in
strong trends — which are exactly the conditions in which you least needed an
indicator to tell you the direction.

## What MACD is actually good for

Given all that, the fairer summary: MACD is a reasonable way to *visualise*
whether momentum is building or fading, and a poor standalone trigger.

The histogram is the genuinely useful part. When bars are growing, the gap
between MACD and its signal is widening — momentum is accelerating. When
they're shrinking, momentum is fading even if price is still rising. That
fading-while-rising condition is real information, and it's visible on the
histogram before it's visible in the crossover.

Zero-line crossings are also more meaningful than signal crossings, simply
because they happen far less often and require an actual shift in the
relationship between the two underlying averages.

## Doing it in Python

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("britannia-ohlcv-2024-04-to-2026-03.csv",
                 parse_dates=["date"]).set_index("date")

ema12 = df.close.ewm(span=12, adjust=False).mean()
ema26 = df.close.ewm(span=26, adjust=False).mean()
df["macd"]   = ema12 - ema26
df["signal"] = df.macd.ewm(span=9, adjust=False).mean()
df["hist"]   = df.macd - df.signal

# count crossovers, skipping the warmup
x = (df.macd > df.signal).astype(int).diff().iloc[35:]
print(f"bullish {int((x == 1).sum())}, bearish {int((x == -1).sum())}")
```

`adjust=False` matters — it's what makes pandas' EMA match the standard
recursive definition every charting package uses. With the default
`adjust=True` you'll get subtly different values and wonder why your numbers
don't match your broker's terminal.

## Common mistakes

- **Trading every crossover.** Thirty-one signals in two years on one stock,
  clustered exactly where they're least informative.
- **Forgetting it's a lagging indicator built from lagging indicators.** Two
  EMAs, then a third EMA of their difference. It is structurally incapable of
  leading price.
- **Comparing MACD values across stocks.** MACD is measured in rupees — it's
  a difference between two prices. A ₹40 MACD on a ₹6,000 stock and on a ₹200
  stock mean completely different things.
- **Quoting values from the warmup.** The 26-day EMA plus a 9-day EMA of the
  result needs roughly 35 bars before it settles.
- **Optimising 12/26/9.** Those numbers are a 1970s convention. Any "better"
  set found by searching past data is mostly fitted to that data's noise.
- **Reading divergence as a timing signal.** As with RSI, divergences describe
  fading momentum and can persist for a long time.

**Takeaway:** MACD plots the gap between a fast and a slow moving average,
then compares that gap to its own average — a clear way to see momentum
building or fading. But it produced 31 crossover signals in under two years
here, bunched precisely in the sideways stretches where they were least
useful, which is why the histogram's shape is worth more than the crossover
it eventually produces.
