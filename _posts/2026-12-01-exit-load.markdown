## What an exit load is

An **exit load** is a charge a [mutual fund]({% post_url 2026-10-18-what-a-mutual-fund-is %}) deducts from the redemption value
when you sell units before a specified holding period — most commonly **1% if
redeemed within 365 days of purchase**, and nothing after. It exists to
discourage hot money: investors who dart in and out force the fund to buy and
sell holdings, and those trading costs land on everyone who stayed.

Two things to know before the arithmetic. The load goes to the *scheme*, not
to the fund house — it is credited back to the fund's assets, so the remaining
investors are compensated, not the AMC (asset management company, the fund
house that runs the scheme). And it is charged on the *value* at
redemption, not the amount you invested.

Load structures vary: liquid funds use a graded load over the first seven
days; many equity funds use 1% within a year; some funds have none at all. The
scheme information document states it, and it can change (for future purchases
only) with notice.

## The formula

```
Exit load (₹)     =  Redemption value of the units × Load %
Amount you get    =  Redemption value − Exit load

"Redemption value" and "within the window" are assessed LOT BY LOT —
each purchase has its own date, so each has its own 365-day clock.
```

Notice this is the same lot-by-lot logic the tax series used in the

instalment is its own purchase with its own date, for tax *and* for load.
Funds apply the same first-in-first-out order, so a partial redemption sells
the oldest — load-free — units first.

## When it matters, and when it doesn't

For a long-term holder the exit load is irrelevant: cross the window and it
vanishes. It matters in three situations:

- **Switching between schemes of the same fund house.** A switch is a
  redemption plus a purchase, so load applies to the exit side.
- **STPs out of a fund** (systematic transfer plans) started within a year of
  the source investment.
- **Panicking.** Selling in a drawdown inside the window turns a paper loss
  into a realised loss *and* pays a percent for the privilege.

It also interacts with tax: the load is deducted from the sale consideration,
so it reduces the capital gain slightly. Small consolation.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking the load applies to the amount invested.** It's on the value at
  redemption — higher if the fund rose, lower if it fell.
- **Assuming a SIP's clock starts at the first instalment.** Each instalment
  has its own clock. Fourteen months in, eleven of these lots were still
  loaded.
- **Forgetting that a switch is a redemption.** Moving from one scheme to
  another within the same AMC still triggers the load on the source.
- **Confusing exit load with expense ratio.** The expense ratio is an ongoing
  annual cost deducted from NAV; the exit load is a one-time deduction on
  early redemption. The
  [expense ratio post]({% post_url 2026-10-21-expense-ratios-direct-vs-regular %})
  covers the first.

**Takeaway:** an exit load is a percentage — typically 1% within a year —
deducted from the redemption value of units sold early, assessed purchase by
purchase. That lot-by-lot rule is why a fourteen-month SIP redeemed at once
still pays load on eleven of its fourteen instalments.
