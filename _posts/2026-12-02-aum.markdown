## What AUM means

**AUM — Assets Under Management** — is the total market value of everything a
mutual fund scheme holds, less its liabilities: all the shares, bonds and cash
in the portfolio, marked to today's prices. It is the size of the pot.

The [first mutual fund post]({% post_url 2026-10-18-what-a-mutual-fund-is %})
defined NAV as that pot divided by the number of units. So:

```
AUM  =  NAV per unit × Units outstanding
```

which means AUM changes for two entirely different reasons: units change
(investors buy or redeem) or NAV changes (the market moves). Fund marketing
tends to present AUM growth as evidence of investor confidence. Often it's
just the index going up.

## Worked example: the market moves the AUM by itself

Take a fund with ₹{% include inr.html n=au.illustrative_aum_start_cr %} crore of AUM at the start of a
financial year and assume *nobody* buys or sells a single unit all year. Its
AUM at year-end is simply the starting AUM times the NAV change. Using the
UTI Nifty 50 Index Fund's actual NAV path (source: AMFI via mfapi.in; data to
31 March 2026):

| Year | NAV change | AUM at year-end, zero flows |

| FY25 (Apr 2024 – Mar 2025) | {{ au.fy25_nav_change_pct }}% | ₹{% include inr.html n=au.fy25_aum_end_no_flows_cr %} crore |
| FY26 (Apr 2025 – Mar 2026) | {{ au.fy26_nav_change_pct }}% | ₹{% include inr.html n=au.fy26_aum_end_no_flows_cr %} crore |

Historical data, for illustration only. The point isn't the fund; it's that a
headline like "AUM fell 4%" or "AUM grew 6%" is uninformative until you know
how much of it was flows. The number that tells you what *investors* did is
**net inflow** — AMFI publishes it monthly, by category — not AUM.

## Where size genuinely matters

**Fees.** SEBI caps a scheme's total expense ratio (TER) in slabs that fall as
AUM rises — the regulator's view being that running a ₹20,000 crore fund
doesn't cost forty times what running a ₹500 crore fund does. For open-ended
equity schemes:

| AUM slab | Maximum TER |
|---|---:|{% for s in au.ter_slabs_equity %}
| {{ s.aum_slab }} | {{ s.max_ter_pct }}% |{% endfor %}

Index funds and ETFs are capped at {{ au.ter_cap_index_funds_pct }}% regardless of size. (Slab
structure per SEBI's TER framework in force since April 2019; the exact
figures are worth confirming against the current regulation before relying
on them.) The practical consequence: as a fund grows, its maximum permitted
fee *falls*. Whether the fund actually charges less is a different question —
the cap is a ceiling, not a price — and the
[expense ratio post]({% post_url 2026-10-21-expense-ratios-direct-vs-regular %})
is where to check what you're really paying.

**Liquidity.** A large fund can't buy meaningful positions in small companies
without moving their prices. A ₹50,000 crore small-cap fund putting 2% into a
₹1,000 crore company would own the whole company. This is why very large
small- and mid-cap funds sometimes stop accepting lump sums, and why "biggest
fund in the category" is not a compliment in every category.

**Nothing else.** AUM says nothing about returns, risk or quality. The
[rolling returns]({% post_url 2026-10-20-rolling-returns %}) and
[drawdown]({% post_url 2026-10-22-drawdown %}) posts don't mention size once,
and that's not an oversight.

AUM is the total weight of everything in a shopkeeper's storeroom.

The storeroom gets heavier when customers bring more stuff in (inflows). But
it also gets "heavier" if the price of everything already inside goes up —
without a single new item arriving. When the shop says "our storeroom grew
6% this year!", ask: did more stuff arrive, or did the old stuff just get
pricier?

## Common mistakes

- **Reading AUM growth as inflows.** Separate the two. In a year the index
  rose 6%, an unchanged fund's AUM rose 6%.
- **Treating big as safe.** Size buys lower fee caps and nothing else. A large
  fund fell exactly as far in 2008 as a small one holding the same stocks.
- **Ignoring size where it bites.** In small- and mid-cap categories, AUM
  relative to the size of the companies held is a real constraint.
- **Comparing AUM across categories.** A ₹5,000 crore liquid fund is small;
  a ₹5,000 crore small-cap fund is large. Same number, opposite meaning.

**Takeaway:** AUM is NAV times units — the market value of a fund's pot — and
it moves with the market whether or not anyone invests a rupee. It sets the
regulator's fee ceiling and constrains what a small-cap fund can buy; it says
nothing about whether the fund is any good.
