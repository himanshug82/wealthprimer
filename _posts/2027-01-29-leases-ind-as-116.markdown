## What Ind AS 116 changed

A company that rents a warehouse for five years has, in substance, borrowed
the warehouse: it has the use of an asset and a fixed obligation to pay for
it. **Ind AS 116** (the Indian Accounting Standard on leases) makes the
renter, the lessee, account for it that way. At the start of the lease it
records a **right-of-use asset** and a **lease liability** (paragraph 22).
The yearly rent disappears from operating costs; in its place come
depreciation on the asset and interest on the liability (paragraphs 31–32
and 36–38), shown separately in the profit and loss (P&L) statement
(paragraph 49).

The rent is the same cash either way. What changes is where it shows up,
and that moves several ratios readers lean on. It's part of
[Fundamental Analysis]({{ '/series/fundamental-analysis/' | relative_url }}).

Two exemptions let a lessee keep the old rent-as-expense treatment: leases
of 12 months or less, and leases of low-value assets (paragraph 5).

## The formula

```
Lease liability at the start  =  present value of the lease payments,
                                 discounted at the rate implicit in the lease
                                 or, failing that, the lessee's incremental
                                 borrowing rate                     (para 26)

Right-of-use asset at the start  ≈  the lease liability (plus any upfront costs)

Each year:
  Interest     =  opening liability × rate
  Liability    =  opening + interest − payment
  Depreciation =  right-of-use asset / lease term    (straight line, in this example)
  P&L charge   =  depreciation + interest            (rent no longer in opex)
```

Discounting is explained in the
[discounting post]({% post_url 2026-09-27-discounting-time-value-of-money %}).

## Worked example: a warehouse lease, on Desi Bites' FY26 numbers

[Desi Bites Foods]({{ '/case-study/' | relative_url }}) is fictional, and
its FY26 accounts have no leases. Suppose it signs one: a warehouse for
{{ l.years }} years at ₹{{ l.rent }} lakh a year, paid at the end of each
year, with an incremental borrowing rate of {{ l.rate_pct }}%. All three
numbers are assumptions.

**Step 1: the liability.** The present value of five payments of
₹{{ l.rent }} lakh at {{ l.rate_pct }}% is ₹{{ l.liability0 }} lakh. That's
the lease liability, and the right-of-use asset starts at the same figure.

**Step 2: the five years.**

| Year | Opening liability | Interest | Payment | Closing liability | Depreciation | P&L charge | Rent, if expensed |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
{% for y in l.schedule -%}
| {{ y.year }} | {{ y.opening }} | {{ y.interest }} | {{ y.payment }} | {{ y.closing }} | {{ y.depreciation }} | {{ y.charge }} | {{ y.rent_charge }} |
{% endfor %}

Over the five years both columns total ₹{{ l.total_charge }} lakh. But the
Ind AS 116 charge is front-loaded: ₹{{ y1.charge }} lakh in year 1, above the
rent, falling to ₹{{ y5.charge }} lakh in year 5, below it. Interest is
highest when the liability is largest.

**Step 3: what year 1 does to the ratios.** Desi Bites' actual FY26 figures
with the lease added, both ways, at the FY26 year-end: in both cases the
year's ₹{{ l.rent }} lakh has been paid from cash, and under Ind AS 116 the
year-end liability is the closing ₹{{ l.liability_close }} lakh. EBITDA is
earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (see the
[EBITDA margin post]({% post_url 2026-08-28-ebitda-margin %})); EBIT is
earnings before interest and tax. Figures in ₹ lakh.

| FY26, ₹ lakh | Rent as an expense | Ind AS 116 |

| EBITDA | {{ rc.ebitda }} | {{ lc.ebitda }} |
| EBITDA margin | {{ rc.ebitda_margin_pct }}% | {{ lc.ebitda_margin_pct }}% |
| EBIT | {{ rc.ebit }} | {{ lc.ebit }} |
| Interest | {{ rc.interest }} | {{ lc.interest }} |
| Interest cover (EBIT ÷ interest) | {{ rc.interest_cover }}× | {{ lc.interest_cover }}× |
| Profit before tax | {{ rc.pbt }} | {{ lc.pbt }} |
| Net debt (debt − cash; negative = net cash) | {{ rc.net_debt | replace: "-", "−" }} | {{ lc.net_debt | replace: "-", "−" }} |
| Cash from operations | {{ rc.cfo }} | {{ lc.cfo }} |

What moved, and why:

1. **EBITDA rose by the whole rent.** Rent left operating costs; depreciation
   and interest are both below EBITDA. The EBITDA margin went from
   {{ rc.ebitda_margin_pct }}% to {{ lc.ebitda_margin_pct }}% with nothing
   about the business changed.
2. **Interest cover fell.** EBIT rose a little (rent out, smaller
   depreciation in), but interest rose by the lease interest, so the
   [interest coverage]({% post_url 2026-09-17-interest-coverage %}) ratio
   dropped from {{ rc.interest_cover }}× to {{ lc.interest_cover }}×.
3. **Debt appeared.** Desi Bites has net cash, and the year-end lease
   liability of ₹{{ l.liability_close }} lakh cut it. A company with net debt would see
   [net debt to EBITDA]({% post_url 2026-09-18-net-debt-ebitda %}) change
   in both its parts at once.
4. **Cash from operations rose by the whole rent.** Ind AS 116's paragraph
   50 puts both parts of each payment, principal and interest, in financing
   cash flows. So operating cash flow is ₹{{ l.rent }} lakh higher; total cash
   out is the same ₹{{ l.rent }} lakh.
5. **Year-1 profit fell slightly.** The P&L charge was
   ₹{{ l.y1_charge_minus_rent }} lakh more than the rent, so profit before
   tax was lower. The charge stays above the rent in years 2 and 3 and
   falls below it from year 4.

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing EBITDA across the change.** A retailer, airline or hospital
   that leases most of its space reports a far higher EBITDA under Ind AS
   116 than under the old rules, or than a peer whose rents stay in operating
   costs (short-term, low-value or sales-linked leases). Against a peer that
   owns its buildings it now looks alike: both carry the cost below EBITDA. The
   sector posts on [telecom]({% post_url 2026-10-23-telecom-arpu-and-the-limits-of-sector-lenses %})
   and [airlines]({% post_url 2026-12-15-airlines-load-factor-rask-cask %})
   show the measures readers and companies use around this, such as EBITDA
   after lease costs and EBITDAR (EBITDA before rentals).
2. **Counting lease liabilities in some ratios and not others.** If EBITDA
   excludes the rent, debt should include the lease liability, and
   [enterprise value]({% post_url 2026-10-13-enterprise-value %}) too.
   Mixing an Ind AS 116 EBITDA with a debt figure that leaves out leases
   flatters the company.
3. **Reading higher operating cash flow as better business.** The same rent
   is still paid; under Ind AS 116 all of it has moved to financing. A free
   cash flow figure that subtracts only capex leaves the lease payments out.
4. **Assuming every lease is on the balance sheet.** Short-term and
   low-value leases can stay as an expense, and variable payments, such as
   rent tied to sales, are generally left out of the liability. The notes
   say which.

**Takeaway:** Ind AS 116 turns a multi-year rent into an asset and a debt,
so the same cash shows up as depreciation and interest instead of rent. EBITDA
rises, interest cover falls and net debt grows, with nothing about the
business changed, so compare companies on the same basis.
