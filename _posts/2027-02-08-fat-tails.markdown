## What are fat tails?

Picture a histogram of daily returns. Most days sit near the middle: small gains, small losses. The **tails** are the two ends, the extreme days. A **fat tail** means those extreme days are more common than the standard bell curve (the **normal distribution**) predicts. If returns were normal, very large moves would be so rare you could ignore them. In real markets you can't.

The usual yardstick is the **standard deviation (SD)**, a measure of how far a typical day strays from the average. Under a bell curve, about 99.7% of days land within 3 SDs of the average, so a move beyond 3 SDs should happen on roughly 1 day in 370. Counting how often it really happens is the test. This post belongs to the [Risk, Leverage and Your Own Brain]({{ '/series/risk/' | relative_url }}) series, and uses the volatility ideas from the [volatility and Sharpe post]({% post_url 2026-09-30-volatility-and-sharpe %}).

## The formula

```
z   = (daily return − average daily return) / SD of daily returns
P(|z| > k) under a normal curve = 2 × (1 − Φ(k))      (Φ = the normal cumulative distribution)

Expected days beyond k SDs  =  P(|z| > k) × number of days
Excess kurtosis             =  a single number for tail fatness; 0 for a normal curve
```

## Why it matters

- **Position sizes and stop distances based on "normal" volatility understate the bad days.** The [position sizing post]({% post_url 2026-10-10-position-sizing-and-the-one-percent-rule %}) used stops based on ATR (average true range). A gap through a stop on a fat-tail day is a loss bigger than the budget.
- **Leverage turns a rare day into an exit.** A 5% fall is survivable unlevered and ruinous at 20× ([leverage and margin]({% post_url 2026-10-11-leverage-and-margin %})).
- **"It's a six-sigma event" is a claim about the model, not about the market.** If a model says a day should essentially never happen and it happens, the model is what is wrong.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a bell curve because the average day looks like one.** Fatness is in the tails, and it is invisible on a chart of ordinary days.
- **Treating one period's volatility as permanent.** Most of the extremes here come from three calendar years.
- **Calling extreme days "outliers" and removing them.** For a risk question, they are the data.
- **Concluding the market is always dangerous.** The same data say most days are quiet. The point is the shape: rare, large and clustered.

## Doing it in Python

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from math import erfc, sqrt

s = pd.read_csv("nifty50-price-index.csv", parse_dates=["date"]
                ).set_index("date").nifty50_pri_close
r = s.pct_change().dropna()
z = (r - r.mean()) / r.std()

for k in (3, 4, 5):
    actual = int((z.abs() > k).sum())
    expected = erfc(k / sqrt(2)) * len(r)       # two-tailed normal probability
    print(k, actual, round(expected, 1))
print("excess kurtosis:", round(r.kurt(), 1))
print(r.nsmallest(5).round(3))
```

**Takeaway:** Extreme market days happen far more often than a bell curve says, and they arrive in clusters during crises. On 18 years of Nifty data, moves beyond 4 SDs happened dozens of times where the bell curve expects almost none, so any plan that assumes normal behaviour is built for a market that hasn't existed.
