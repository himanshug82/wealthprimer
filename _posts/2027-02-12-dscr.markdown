## What DSCR means

The **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)** compares what a business earns in
cash terms with its **debt service**: everything it must pay lenders in the
year, both interest and the principal repayments falling due.
[Interest coverage]({% post_url 2026-09-17-interest-coverage %}) asks only
whether profit covers the interest. DSCR asks whether it covers the
repayments too, which is the question a lender cares about when a loan is
being paid down in instalments.

A DSCR above 1 means the year's earnings covered the year's debt service;
below 1, the gap had to come from elsewhere: cash in hand, new borrowing, asset
sales or new equity. Lenders
define the numerator in different ways, so this post shows two. It's part of
[Jargon, Decoded]({{ '/series/jargon/' | relative_url }}).

## The formula

```
Debt service  =  interest  +  principal repaid in the year

DSCR (a common lender version)  =  (PAT + depreciation and amortisation + interest) ÷ debt service
DSCR (EBITDA version)           =  EBITDA ÷ debt service
```

PAT is profit after tax ([net margin]({% post_url 2026-08-30-net-margin %})
explains it); [EBITDA]({% post_url 2026-08-28-ebitda-margin %}) is earnings
before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. The first version adds back the non-cash
depreciation and the interest that's being covered; the second starts before
tax. Some lenders also use cash from operations, or subtract tax and capex.
The definition in a loan agreement is the one that counts for that loan.

**Why DSCR is lower than interest cover.** Interest cover divides by
interest alone. Adding the principal more than doubles the denominator, while
the numerator rises only modestly, so the cover is smaller.

**What the two versions pick up.** Because it starts from PAT, the lender
version includes FY26's ₹{{ b.other_income }} lakh of other income (interest
on the IPO cash) and the ₹{{ b.exceptional | abs }} lakh one-off cost; the
EBITDA version includes neither. Taking the other income out, after tax, the
lender version would be {{ b.dscr_lender_ex_oi }}×. FY25 had neither item.

**What a bullet repayment does.** Suppose that in a later year, with FY26's
earnings and interest, the remaining ₹{{ d.bullet.repaid }} lakh of the term
loan fell due at once instead of in instalments. Debt service would be
₹{{ d.bullet.service }} lakh and the lender-version DSCR
{{ d.bullet.dscr_lender }}×. The business is the same; the repayment
schedule changed the ratio. With its IPO (initial public offering) cash,
Desi Bites could pay either way; a company without the cash would need to
refinance or find it elsewhere.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using interest cover when the loan amortises.** A company can cover its
   interest comfortably and still struggle to meet instalments. DSCR is the
   test for repayments.
2. **Comparing DSCRs built differently.** One source's DSCR may start from
   EBITDA, another's from cash flow after tax. Check the formula before
   comparing.
3. **Reading one year's DSCR as the loan's safety.** Repayment schedules
   change: a bullet year can pull DSCR down sharply even when the business
   hasn't changed, as the example shows.

**Takeaway:** DSCR asks whether a year's earnings cover everything owed to
lenders, interest and principal, not just the interest. Lenders define it in
different ways, and a lumpy repayment year can move it far more than the
business does.
